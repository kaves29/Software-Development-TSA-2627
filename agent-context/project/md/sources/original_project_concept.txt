Project Concept: Speakly — Personalized Communication Training
Working title · TSA Software Development 2026–2027
1. The core idea
SpeakWise is an AI-assisted communication training platform that helps people of different ages practice speaking, presentations, interviews, and everyday communication through personalized feedback, optional body-language analysis, and adaptive exercises.
Many people need to develop communication skills throughout their lives. A student may struggle with classroom presentations, a job seeker may want to practice interviews, an employee may need to communicate more clearly in meetings, or someone may simply want a comfortable environment to practice speaking.
Professional coaching can be expensive or difficult to access, and generic practice tools don't necessarily adapt to individual needs.
SpeakWise addresses this by allowing users to practice on their own schedule, receive feedback about observable communication patterns, and follow exercises tailored to their goals.
The app does not attempt to determine whether someone is inherently a good speaker. Instead, it helps people identify skills they want to practice and gives them tools to work on those skills.
2. The problem we're solving
Communication is a skill people develop across different stages of life, but opportunities to practice aren't always accessible.
Limited practice opportunities: People may not have someone available to listen to repeated practice attempts.
Generic feedback: Many tools provide a transcript or a score without explaining what the user should practice next.
Expensive coaching: Individualized instruction may not be affordable or readily available to everyone.
Difficulty recognizing patterns: Speakers may not notice recurring filler words, pacing patterns, long pauses, or movement habits.
Different needs and goals: Someone preparing for an interview needs different practice from someone working on a presentation or everyday conversation.
SpeakWise combines speech analysis, optional movement awareness, and a personalized learning system to address these challenges.
3. How the application works
Step 1 — Choose a goal
Users choose what they want to practice, their scenario, and which feedback they want. For example, someone might select interview practice and choose speaking pace as a goal.
Step 2 — Complete a practice session
Users respond to a prompt by recording audio, with optional webcam video when movement feedback is enabled.
Step 3 — Review communication patterns
The app analyzes available speech metrics and, when enabled, observable movement patterns. It presents the findings with explanations and limitations rather than treating them as definitive judgments.
Step 4 — Receive a personalized exercise
Your own software selects a relevant activity based on the user's chosen goal, previous attempts, and available feedback.
Step 5 — Practice again and track progress
Users complete another attempt, compare similar sessions, and review their progress toward personal goals.
4. The major features
A. Speech analysis
The application records a user's response and uses speech-processing tools to extract useful information.
Possible metrics include:
Speaking pace, measured in words per minute when a reliable transcript and duration are available.
Pauses and their approximate durations.
Filler-word frequency, such as repeated use of “um” or “like,” where transcription supports reliable detection.
Transcript review to help users notice unclear explanations or overly long responses.
Optional speech-fluency practice activities selected by the user.
Important distinction: ordinary speech-to-text APIs do not automatically provide reliable stuttering detection. SpeakWise can offer supportive fluency-practice activities without claiming to diagnose or treat a speech disorder.
B. Body-language and movement awareness
This is the webcam feature you mentioned.
Using a computer-vision pose-estimation model, the application can identify body landmarks and summarize certain observable movement patterns.
Possible features include:
Detecting repeated upper-body movement during a recording.
Showing an approximate visualization of body position.
Helping users review movement patterns during presentation practice.
Allowing users to select a specific movement-related goal.
Comparing observable patterns between practice attempts.
The app should not assume that a certain posture means someone is confident, nervous, dishonest, or competent.
For example, instead of saying, “Your body language is bad,” it could say, “Your upper-body position changed several times during this recording.” The user can then review the recording and decide whether that movement is relevant to their own goals.
Movement tracking should be optional, and users should still be able to use the app without a webcam.
C. Multiple practice modes

Presentation mode
Practice explaining topics, organizing responses, maintaining a suitable pace, and reviewing optional movement feedback.

Interview mode
Generate practice questions, record responses, review answer structure, and practice giving relevant, concise answers.

Everyday communication mode
Practice explaining ideas, responding to conversational prompts, and communicating information clearly.

Personalized fluency practice
Users choose supportive speaking exercises at their own pace. This mode avoids diagnosing speech disorders or presenting disfluency as a personal failure.
These modes serve different ages and situations while sharing the same underlying analysis and learning system.
D. The personalized exercise engine — the most important feature
This is what makes SpeakWise more than a collection of existing APIs.
After every session, the software evaluates the user's selected goals and available metrics to recommend what they should practice next.
For example:
A user selects interview practice and wants to work on pacing.
They record an answer.
The app estimates speaking pace and identifies available feedback.
The exercise engine checks the user's target and previous sessions.
It recommends an exercise, such as answering a short question while practicing a self-selected pacing goal.
The user completes another attempt and compares the results.
The engine could eventually consider several goals at once, prioritize the user's most relevant practice area, and adjust exercise difficulty as the user progresses.
The initial version does not need an advanced machine-learning model for this. A well-designed rule-based recommendation algorithm is a legitimate algorithm you can build, test, document, and explain. You can explore more advanced personalization later.
E. Progress dashboard
Users can view their practice history and progress across different metrics.
Examples include:
Speaking pace across comparable attempts.
Filler-word frequency across sessions.
Completion of chosen practice exercises.
Self-reported comfort with a specific communication task.
Movement summaries when the user has enabled camera analysis.
Avoid combining all these measurements into one universal “communication ability” score. A user might improve their pacing while still preferring a different speaking style, and both can be perfectly reasonable.
F. Personalized prompt generation
The app can generate practice prompts based on the user's scenario and goals.
Examples:
Explain a complicated concept in 60 seconds.
Answer a behavioral interview question.
Give a short persuasive presentation.
Explain a process to someone unfamiliar with the topic.
Prompts can gradually change in difficulty, while the app reuses the same or similar prompts when comparisons between attempts are needed.
Generated feedback and exercises should be reviewed against clear rules so that the app does not invent problems or make unsupported judgments.

Speech transcription.
Pose-estimation model.
Optional language model for generating prompts.
Our original software:
The application that connects all components.
The logic that calculates and organizes useful metrics.
The goal-selection and exercise recommendation algorithm.
The progress-tracking system.
The practice modes and their workflows.
The interface that communicates feedback responsibly.
The testing system that evaluates functionality and usefulness.
Using existing APIs is normal software engineering. However, simply sending a recording to an API and displaying its output would be too limited. Our own learning system must be the centerpiece.
6. How we handle bias, accessibility, and privacy
This should be part of the design from the beginning, not a disclaimer added at the end.
Bias and different communication styles
Do not infer confidence, intelligence, honesty, or competence from voice or posture.
Let users choose their goals instead of imposing one ideal speaking style.
Treat movement feedback as descriptive and optional.
Test transcription with different accents and recording conditions, and report limitations honestly.
Allow users to disregard feedback that does not match their situation.
Accessibility
Support keyboard navigation and readable text.
Allow practice without a webcam.
Provide written prompts and transcripts where possible.
Avoid making speed, eye contact, or stillness requirements for all users.
Make practice approachable for users with different needs.
Privacy
Explain what data is processed and stored.
Avoid storing raw audio and video unless genuinely needed and explicitly consented to.
Give users control over their saved session history.
Use secure handling for API credentials and user data.
For a student competition, it would be sensible to begin with limited account data or a local prototype and use consent-based testing.
7. How we prove the project actually helps
A major strength would be testing SpeakWise rather than merely claiming it works.
We could conduct a small, voluntary usability study with participants who agree to try the application.
A possible research question is:
Does personalized, goal-based speaking practice help users improve their selected communication metrics more effectively than receiving generic feedback?
A simple study could involve:
Participants complete a baseline practice task.
One group receives generic feedback, while another receives goal-based exercises, if the study can be organized fairly.
Participants complete comparable practice tasks.
We compare relevant metrics and collect feedback about usability and perceived usefulness.
A small study cannot establish broad effectiveness for everyone. We would report the sample size, limitations, and results honestly.
We should also test the software itself: transcription failures, camera permission issues, API errors, missing audio, inaccurate metrics, and database behavior. Automated unit tests would help demonstrate strong coding practices to TSA judges.

9. A realistic development roadmap
Do not try to build every feature at once. The best strategy is to make the core workflow work first, then add complexity.
Phase 1 — Build the basic web app
Create the interface, practice modes, recording screen, goal selection, and session structure.
Phase 2 — Integrate speech analysis
Connect a speech API, process transcripts, and implement your own supported metrics. Add error handling for failed recordings and API requests.
Phase 3 — Build the personalized learning engine
Implement the algorithm that uses user-selected goals and prior results to recommend targeted exercises.
Phase 4 — Add session history and progress
Save relevant metrics and build a dashboard for comparing practice attempts.
Phase 5 — Add optional pose estimation
Implement webcam permissions, landmark visualization, movement summaries, and controls to disable camera processing.
Phase 6 — Test, document, and polish
Test edge cases, improve accessibility, conduct a small user evaluation, document the code, and prepare an offline demonstration fallback.
A focused minimum viable product could take several weeks, while a polished version with reliable speech analysis, movement tracking, testing, and documentation could take considerably longer. Your experience, team size, API setup, and available time will determine the actual schedule.
